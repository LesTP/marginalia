Here are eight observations on the opening of Ecclesiastes:

1.  The opening declaration, "Vanity of vanities," uses a grammatical structure common in its source language (a noun followed by the same noun in the genitive case) to create an absolute superlative. It does not merely say things are vain, but that they are the very essence and peak of vanity, establishing an uncompromising and totalizing tone from the outset.

2.  The argument is built through a progression from a central question ("What profit...?") to a series of concrete, cyclical images from nature (generations, sun, wind, rivers). These specific examples of ceaseless, unfulfilling motion then support the broader, more abstract pronouncements about human labor and perception that follow, giving the philosophy a grounding in observable phenomena.

3.  The syntax mimics the circularity it describes, particularly in verses 5-7. The repeated use of "and" (a rhetorical device known as polysyndeton) and the looping sentence structures—like the sun hastening "to his place where he arose"—create a monotonous, flowing rhythm that makes the sound of the text enact its own argument about endless repetition.

4.  The imagery of the natural world is not serene but characterized by frantic, pointless motion. The sun "hasteth to his place" and the wind "whirleth about continually," suggesting a kind of compulsory, exhausting activity rather than a peaceful, ordered cycle. This sense of drivenness reinforces the thesis that "All things are full of labour."

5.  The recurring phrase "under the sun" acts as a crucial qualifier, defining the precise scope of the Preacher’s critique. It brackets the entire argument within the realm of earthly, mortal experience, relentlessly denying meaning within this observable world while implicitly leaving open the question of what might exist beyond it.

6.  There is a distinct structural pivot in verse 8 from observing external phenomena to diagnosing internal human experience. After demonstrating the futility in nature, the text turns inward to the insatiability of the senses ("the eye is not satisfied with seeing"), arguing that the problem lies not just in the world's emptiness but in our own perceptual and psychological constitution.

7.  The choice of "vanity" to translate the Hebrew key word (*hevel*) is significant in English. While it means futility, it also carries older connotations of vapor, breath, or a puff of smoke. This more physical sense enriches the reading, suggesting that all things are not just pointless but also ephemeral, insubstantial, and fleeting.

8.  The passage concludes by universalizing its argument across time, moving from the present ("The thing that hath been, it is that which shall be") to the past and future in verse 11. The final, crushing point is not just that striving is futile, but that it is also forgotten, denying even the consolation of leaving a legacy.
