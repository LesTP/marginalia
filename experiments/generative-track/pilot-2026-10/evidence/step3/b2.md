Strong-lines is right to insist that the passage is an argument, not just an ornament. The syntax really does reason its way through separation: “If they be two” grants the feared premise, then tries to make even that premise harmless. The beloved may be distinct from the speaker, but the distinction is reimagined as the two legs of one instrument. That is a genuine argumentative move, and the passage depends on it.

But I would resist the claim that the conceit therefore becomes wholly triumphant, or that its coldness is simply its strength. The stanza is moving, but it is also stiff in more than one sense. The word “stiff” is on the page, and it matters. Donne does not merely choose a compass; he chooses “stiff twin compasses.” The image asks us to accept that lovers at parting are best understood through a hard, jointed measuring tool. That is clever; it is not obviously tender. The wit does emotional work, yes, but it also displaces emotion into mechanism.

Where strong-lines is strongest is on the fixed foot. The beloved is not inert. The lines say:

> “makes no show / To move, but doth, if th’other do.”

That is a fine paradox: visible stillness, hidden motion. And “leans, and hearkens after it” is the best softening of the conceit. “Hearkens” humanizes the fixed foot; it gives it attention, yearning, inwardness. The beloved is not policing the speaker’s motion. She is responsive to it. On this point, the reading is persuasive: the conceit “cracks open,” as strong-lines says, and feeling enters.

But I think strong-lines overstates when they say the conceit has “earned the right” to be carnal in “grows erect, as that comes home.” The phrase undeniably has an erotic possibility. “Grows erect” and “comes home” are too charged to be treated as merely technical. Yet to make that erotic charge “structurally necessary” risks making the line smoother than it is. The phrase is awkward because it must serve too many masters at once: compass movement, reunion, bodily desire, spiritual reassurance. That compression is witty, but it is also slightly indecorous. At a moment of parting, the sudden bodily innuendo can feel less like consummated tenderness than like wit refusing to keep its hands still.

The largest disagreement is over the final two lines:

> “Thy firmness makes my circle just,  
> And makes me end, where I begun.”

Strong-lines reads this as triumph: the circle is perfect, “just” is exact and morally right, and returning to the beginning is fulfillment. Much of that is textually defensible. “Just” plainly carries the sense of exactness, and it also invites moral approval. The beloved’s “firmness” gives the speaker’s wandering shape and legitimacy. Without the fixed foot, the other foot would not draw a proper circle.

But “end, where I begun” still has a subdued melancholy that the triumphal reading wants to cancel. The speaker does not say he arrives somewhere new. He says he ends where he began. The journey has produced a figure, not an advance. Strong-lines is right that circular form is not nothing; the poem values form. But the line also admits that the whole motion of absence has been a disciplined return to the point of departure. The gain is not experience, enlargement, or discovery. The gain is that separation has not destroyed the original bond.

That is consolation, but it is a modest consolation. The stanza does not abolish loss; it drafts loss into a diagram.

So the clash reveals the double nature of the passage. On one side, the compass conceit is brilliantly exact: two joined things, one fixed and one roaming, separation governed by relation, movement made meaningful by constancy. On the other side, the very exactness is suspect. Grief at parting is being managed by geometry. The beloved becomes “the fixed foot”; the speaker becomes “th’other foot”; fidelity becomes a “just” circle. The feeling is real, but it has been cooled and constrained.

That is why the stanza is powerful and not, to my ear, wholly triumphant. Its success depends on the strain. The image reassures by saying: even distance can be made into unity. But it also betrays the need for reassurance. If the lovers’ union were as serene as the argument wants it to be, the poem would not need so elaborate a machine to prove it.
