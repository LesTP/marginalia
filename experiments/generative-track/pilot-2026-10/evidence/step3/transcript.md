## A opening (strong-lines)

# Opening Reading: The Compass as Argument Made Flesh

What we witness in these three stanzas is Donne at his most characteristic and most triumphant: the full force of intellection pressed into the service of feeling, so that the conceit does not illustrate the emotion but *becomes* it. Let me lay out my reading in points.

**1. The conceit's power lies precisely in its resistance to sentiment.** A compass is a cold, rigid, mathematical instrument — "stiff twin compasses." Donne does not reach for roses or stars. He reaches for a draftsman's tool, and the sheer intellectual audacity of that choice is what makes the tenderness land. The stiffness of the instrument is the stiffness of constancy; the geometry of the circle is the geometry of fidelity. Thought and feeling are not merely yoked — they are fused at the joint, literally, at the pivot where the two legs meet.

**2. R1 is right that "If they be two" is a concession, but wrong to call it a "fallback."** It is a dialectical move — the structure of argument. Donne has already claimed the lovers' souls are one; now he entertains the worst case ("if" they are separable) and demonstrates that *even then* the separation serves unity. This is not retreat; it is the logician's devastating "even if." The conditional strengthens the claim rather than weakening it, because it shows the conclusion holds under the least favorable premise.

**3. The fixed foot is not passive but dynamically responsive, and this is where the conceit earns its greatness.** "Makes no show / To move, but doth, if th'other do" — the negation folding into affirmation within a single clause enacts the paradox of faithful waiting: apparent stillness that is in fact motion. Then "leans, and hearkens after it" — and here R3 is exactly right to note the auditory register. "Hearkens" gives the fixed foot *interiority*. A compass leg cannot listen; by saying it does, Donne smuggles the full weight of longing into a geometric object. The conceit cracks open just enough to let the human rush in.

**4. R4 identifies the erotic charge of "grows erect, as that comes home," and I would press harder on it.** The sexual undertone is not incidental decoration; it is structurally necessary. The entire poem has been arguing that spiritual union survives physical absence. At the moment of return — "comes home" — the erotic body reasserts itself, and the compass image, which seemed so coolly abstract, reveals that it has been encoding bodily desire all along. The erectness is both the compass leg straightening and the body's response to reunion. Donne's wit holds both meanings in perfect tension without embarrassment, because the conceit has *earned* the right to be carnal by first being so rigorously intellectual.

**5. Against R2, I argue that "end where I begun" is not melancholic but triumphant.** R2 reads "no progress, only form" as a diminishment. But that misunderstands what the poem values. The circle is the perfect figure — no beginning, no end, no defect. "Just" (and R5 is right about its double force: exact and righteous) means the roaming foot's journey has produced something: a *just circle*, a completed form. The progress is not linear; it is formal. And in Donne's world, form *is* meaning. The lover returns not unchanged but having inscribed fidelity into the shape of his journey. That the end coincides with the beginning is not futility — it is perfection. Only because "thy firmness makes my circle just" does the return have moral and aesthetic weight. Without the fixed foot, the roaming foot draws nothing — a meaningless arc. The circle is the joint achievement of both lovers, and that is the poem's argument made visible as geometry.

**6. The final couplet's power is that it resolves argument into image and image into promise.** "Thy firmness makes my circle just, / And makes me end, where I begun." Two uses of "makes" — the beloved's constancy is the *cause* of the speaker's perfection. This is not flattery; it is logical demonstration dressed as love lyric. The speaker has proven, through the sustained pressure of the conceit, that fidelity is not merely a feeling but a structure — as real and as demonstrable as the circle a compass draws. That is what the strong line does: it makes you *think* your way into feeling, and by the time you arrive, the feeling is unassailable because it has been argued into existence.

This is the compass conceit's genius: it does not ask you to feel first and understand later. It asks you to understand, and discovers that understanding *is* the feeling.

## B opening (plain-style)

1. **The stanza treats separation as a problem in drafting, and that is its chief weakness.**  
   The lovers are not first presented as grieving bodies or even as speaking minds, but as “stiff twin compasses.” The adjective “stiff” is telling: it suits the instrument, but it chills the feeling. A farewell becomes a demonstration. The emotional burden of distance is translated into fixed foot, moving foot, center, circle, and return. The ingenuity is clear, but the decorum feels strained: the occasion of parting is made to serve a clever mechanical likeness.

2. **R1 is right that “If they be two” is concessive, but the concession does not fully save the image.**  
   “If they be two” does soften the claim. It says, in effect: even if we grant that separation makes us two, we remain joined in another way. But the stanza then leans so heavily into the compass that the concession becomes the main performance. The emotional reassurance depends on accepting the apparatus. Instead of feeling that the lovers’ unity survives distance, we are asked to admire how neatly the parts correspond.

3. **The fixed beloved is given constancy, but also an oddly passive role.**  
   “Thy soul the fixed foot, makes no show / To move, but doth, if th’other do.” This is meant as praise: she is firm, centered, faithful. Yet the phrasing reduces her motion to reaction. She “makes no show” of moving; she moves only because “th’other” moves. Even “leans, and hearkens after it” gives her attention, as R3 says, rather than surveillance. That is a gentler reading, and “hearkens” is one of the warmer words in the passage. Still, she remains imagined as an instrument responding to the man’s roaming.

4. **R4 identifies the boldness of “grows erect,” but that boldness is part of the indecorum.**  
   The line “And grows erect, as that comes home” does bring together the compass closing and bodily reunion. But the fusion is abrupt. In a farewell poem, the erotic charge of “grows erect” risks sounding too knowing, almost too pleased with itself. The phrase pulls attention away from grief or tenderness and toward the poet’s dexterity in making geometry intimate. The result is not simple warmth but a somewhat showy compression.

5. **R5 is persuasive: “just” carries both exactness and moral rightness.**  
   “Thy firmness makes my circle just” is the best defense of the conceit. The beloved’s steadiness makes his wandering not random but ordered; not betrayal but fidelity. “Just” lets the poem claim that emotional truth and formal exactness coincide. Still, the word also reveals the poem’s governing preference: love is vindicated by correctness of figure. The heart is made to pass through mathematical proof.

6. **R2 captures the subdued melancholy of the ending, perhaps more than the poem intends.**  
   “And makes me end, where I begun” promises return, but it also admits circularity. He comes back to the starting point; the motion has produced a “circle,” not growth. The line is reassuring if the main value is fidelity: he ends at home. But it is also bleakly formal. All the distance, all the “obliquely run,” has yielded a completed shape. The stanza consoles by saying the journey will close properly; it does not make the parting feel less barren.

## A rebuttal

# The Compass as Thinking Made Passionate

My interlocutor reads with real care, and I want to honor that before I contest the core of the argument. But the core must be contested, because it rests on a premise I think the stanza itself refutes: that ingenuity and feeling are in competition, that where wit enters, warmth exits. That premise is not self-evident. It is a taste posing as a principle. Let me take the points in order.

---

## Where They Are Right

**On point 3, about the fixed foot's passivity, there is genuine tension.** "Makes no show / To move, but doth, if th'other do" does grammatically subordinate the beloved's motion to the speaker's. Her leaning is reactive; her hearkening follows his roaming. I concede that the stanza distributes agency unevenly. The speaker assigns himself the active, "obliquely" running foot and gives the beloved the role of patient center. If we are asking what imaginative space the beloved occupies, she is praised for constancy — which is real praise — but not for initiative. That is worth noticing.

**On point 5, they are entirely right**, and generous to the poem: "just" is the stanza's hinge, and they see clearly that it lets moral and geometric meaning coincide. I would only add that this is not incidental but *the point*. The word "just" is where the conceit earns its keep, where the apparatus ceases to be apparatus and becomes argument about love's nature. More on this below.

**On R1 and point 2, the concessive force of "If they be two" is well identified.** The stanza does begin from a fallback position. The poem has already argued for the unity of their souls; this is the contingency plan. I grant that.

---

## Where They Are Wrong

**Point 1 is wrong at its root.** "Stiff" does not chill the feeling; it *is* the feeling, or rather the condition that makes feeling trustworthy. The whole emotional argument of the stanza depends on stiffness — on the joint that holds, on the resistance of the hinge to collapse. A compass whose legs were loose would draw nothing. "Stiff" is not an unfortunate mechanical residue; it is the physical correlative of fidelity under strain. The interlocutor says "a farewell becomes a demonstration," as though demonstration were the enemy of consolation. But for a mind like this speaker's, demonstration *is* consolation. The stanza does not translate grief into geometry to evade it; it discovers in geometry a structure adequate to grief. That is not the same thing.

**Point 2 claims the concession "becomes the main performance" and that we are "asked to admire how neatly the parts correspond."** But look at what the stanza actually does after "If they be two." It does not stay in the drafting-room. It moves from the static image of two joined legs to *leaning*, to *hearkening*, to *growing erect*, to *coming home*. The conceit is not displayed and then admired; it is *inhabited* and made to move through time, through the stages of parting, absence, longing, and return. The compass does not sit still on the page as a diagram. It enacts a narrative of separation and reunion. That is precisely what distinguishes a living conceit from a dead one: it sustains temporal development. The interlocutor treats the image as if Donne had simply said "we are like a compass" and stopped. He did not stop. He made the compass feel.

**Point 4 is where I most strongly disagree.** They call "grows erect" indecorous, "too knowing, almost too pleased with itself." But consider what the line actually achieves. The compass leg rises to vertical as the other leg returns; the beloved's body rises in anticipation of reunion. The two meanings do not compete — they *fuse*, and the fusion is the point. The erotic is not dragged in as a wink; it emerges naturally from the conceit's own logic. A compass leg *does* grow erect as the other closes. A lover's body *does* respond to the beloved's return. That these two truths occupy the same words simultaneously is not showing off; it is the discovery that physical law and physical desire share a grammar. If that is indecorum, then indecorum is the highest reach of metaphysical style, because it refuses to separate the body from the mind, the instrument from the desire.

And notice: the interlocutor says the phrase "pulls attention away from grief or tenderness." But "grows erect, as that comes home" is one of the most tender moments in the stanza. "Comes home" is not geometric language at all — it is domestic, intimate, warm. The line moves *from* geometry *toward* the body *toward* the household in a single arc. To call that showy is to have missed its direction of travel.

**Point 6 misreads the circularity.** "End where I begun" is not bleak formalism or barren repetition. It is *promise*. The speaker is leaving. He is saying: I will come back. The circle is not a figure of futility; it is a figure of return, of covenant. My interlocutor reads "no progress, only form" and finds melancholy. But what would progress look like in a farewell poem? The speaker going somewhere new and never returning? The whole emotional need of the occasion is precisely that the journey will close, that the roaming will not be random or endless but will bend back to its origin. "Just" certifies that return. The circle's closure is not emptiness; it is the shape of kept faith. R2 captures something real — there is a formal austerity to the ending — but to call it melancholy is to impose on the poem a Romantic expectation of growth or transformation that the poem explicitly refuses. Donne's value here is not progress; it is constancy. And constancy *is* the circle.

---

## What the Clash Reveals

The deepest disagreement is about whether thought and feeling can occupy the same space. My interlocutor keeps finding moments where ingenuity displaces emotion — "stiff" chills, "grows erect" distracts, the circle empties. In every case, the complaint is the same: the mind is too visibly at work, and therefore the heart must be absent.

But the stanza's entire wager is that this is false. The compass conceit does not *replace* the emotional situation; it *thinks through* it. The fixed foot does not merely represent the beloved — it discovers something about what faithfulness feels like from the inside: the involuntary lean, the listening, the bodily quickening at return. These are not observations imported from outside the conceit; they are generated *by* it. The image thinks, and what it thinks is feeling.

This is what the strong line does. It does not ornament an emotion that exists prior to language; it creates an emotion that could not exist without precisely this language, this conceit, this compression. "Thy firmness makes my circle just" is not a proposition decorated with feeling or a feeling decorated with a proposition. It is both at once, indivisibly. The beloved's steadfastness and the geometric proof are the same act of mind. To separate them — to say "yes, clever, but where is the warmth?" — is to refuse the poem on its own terms.

My interlocutor writes well and reads honestly. But the governing assumption — that the heart must pass through mathematical proof and is diminished by the passage — is exactly what the stanza exists to disprove. The proof *is* the passion. The circle *is* the embrace.

## B rebuttal

Strong-lines is right to insist that the passage is an argument, not just an ornament. The syntax really does reason its way through separation: “If they be two” grants the feared premise, then tries to make even that premise harmless. The beloved may be distinct from the speaker, but the distinction is reimagined as the two legs of one instrument. That is a genuine argumentative move, and the passage depends on it.

But I would resist the claim that the conceit therefore becomes wholly triumphant, or that its coldness is simply its strength. The stanza is moving, but it is also stiff in more than one sense. The word “stiff” is on the page, and it matters. Donne does not merely choose a compass; he chooses “stiff twin compasses.” The image asks us to accept that lovers at parting are best understood through a hard, jointed measuring tool. That is clever; it is not obviously tender. The wit does emotional work, yes, but it also displaces emotion into mechanism.

Where strong-lines is strongest is on the fixed foot. The beloved is not inert. The lines say:

> “makes no show / To move, but doth, if th’other do.”

That is a fine paradox: visible stillness, hidden motion. And “leans, and hearkens after it” is the best softening of the conceit. “Hearkens” humanizes the fixed foot; it gives it attention, yearning, inwardness. The beloved is not policing the speaker’s motion. She is responsive to it. On this point, the reading is persuasive: the conceit “cracks open,” as strong-lines says, and feeling enters.

But I think strong-lines overstates when they say the conceit has “earned the right” to be carnal in “grows erect, as that comes home.” The phrase undeniably has an erotic possibility. “Grows erect” and “comes home” are too charged to be treated as merely technical. Yet to make that erotic charge “structurally necessary” risks making the line smoother than it is. The phrase is awkward because it must serve too many masters at once: compass movement, reunion, bodily desire, spiritual reassurance. That compression is witty, but it is also slightly indecorous. At a moment of parting, the sudden bodily innuendo can feel less like consummated tenderness than like wit refusing to keep its hands still.

The largest disagreement is over the final two lines:

> “Thy firmness makes my circle just,  
> And makes me end, where I begun.”

Strong-lines reads this as triumph: the circle is perfect, “just” is exact and morally right, and returning to the beginning is fulfillment. Much of that is textually defensible. “Just” plainly carries the sense of exactness, and it also invites moral approval. The beloved’s “firmness” gives the speaker’s wandering shape and legitimacy. Without the fixed foot, the other foot would not draw a proper circle.

But “end, where I begun” still has a subdued melancholy that the triumphal reading wants to cancel. The speaker does not say he arrives somewhere new. He says he ends where he began. The journey has produced a figure, not an advance. Strong-lines is right that circular form is not nothing; the poem values form. But the line also admits that the whole motion of absence has been a disciplined return to the point of departure. The gain is not experience, enlargement, or discovery. The gain is that separation has not destroyed the original bond.

That is consolation, but it is a modest consolation. The stanza does not abolish loss; it drafts loss into a diagram.

So the clash reveals the double nature of the passage. On one side, the compass conceit is brilliantly exact: two joined things, one fixed and one roaming, separation governed by relation, movement made meaningful by constancy. On the other side, the very exactness is suspect. Grief at parting is being managed by geometry. The beloved becomes “the fixed foot”; the speaker becomes “th’other foot”; fidelity becomes a “just” circle. The feeling is real, but it has been cooled and constrained.

That is why the stanza is powerful and not, to my ear, wholly triumphant. Its success depends on the strain. The image reassures by saying: even distance can be made into unity. But it also betrays the need for reassurance. If the lovers’ union were as serene as the argument wants it to be, the poem would not need so elaborate a machine to prove it.
