1. **The stanza treats separation as a problem in drafting, and that is its chief weakness.**  
   The lovers are not first presented as grieving bodies or even as speaking minds, but as “stiff twin compasses.” The adjective “stiff” is telling: it suits the instrument, but it chills the feeling. A farewell becomes a demonstration. The emotional burden of distance is translated into fixed foot, moving foot, center, circle, and return. The ingenuity is clear, but the decorum feels strained: the occasion of parting is made to serve a clever mechanical likeness.

2. **R1 is right that “If they be two” is concessive, but the concession does not fully save the image.**  
   “If they be two” does soften the claim. It says, in effect: even if we grant that separation makes us two, we remain joined in another way. But the stanza then leans so heavily into the compass that the concession becomes the main performance. The emotional reassurance depends on accepting the apparatus. Instead of feeling that the lovers’ unity survives distance, we are asked to admire how neatly the parts correspond.

3. **The fixed beloved is given constancy, but also an oddly passive role.**  
   “Thy soul the fixed foot, makes no show / To move, but doth, if th’other do.” This is meant as praise: she is firm, centered, faithful. Yet the phrasing reduces her motion to reaction. She “makes no show” of moving; she moves only because “th’other” moves. Even “leans, and hearkens after it” gives her attention, as R3 says, rather than surveillance. That is a gentler reading, and “hearkens” is one of the warmer words in the passage. Still, she remains imagined as an instrument responding to the man’s roaming.

4. **R4 identifies the boldness of “grows erect,” but that boldness is part of the indecorum.**  
   The line “And grows erect, as that comes home” does bring together the compass closing and bodily reunion. But the fusion is abrupt. In a farewell poem, the erotic charge of “grows erect” risks sounding too knowing, almost too pleased with itself. The phrase pulls attention away from grief or tenderness and toward the poet’s dexterity in making geometry intimate. The result is not simple warmth but a somewhat showy compression.

5. **R5 is persuasive: “just” carries both exactness and moral rightness.**  
   “Thy firmness makes my circle just” is the best defense of the conceit. The beloved’s steadiness makes his wandering not random but ordered; not betrayal but fidelity. “Just” lets the poem claim that emotional truth and formal exactness coincide. Still, the word also reveals the poem’s governing preference: love is vindicated by correctness of figure. The heart is made to pass through mathematical proof.

6. **R2 captures the subdued melancholy of the ending, perhaps more than the poem intends.**  
   “And makes me end, where I begun” promises return, but it also admits circularity. He comes back to the starting point; the motion has produced a “circle,” not growth. The line is reassuring if the main value is fidelity: he ends at home. But it is also bleakly formal. All the distance, all the “obliquely run,” has yielded a completed shape. The stanza consoles by saying the journey will close properly; it does not make the parting feel less barren.
